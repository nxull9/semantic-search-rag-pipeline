The Solar System formed about 4.6 billion years ago from a giant cloud of gas and dust that collapsed under its own gravity. Most of the material gathered at the centre to form the Sun, which today holds about 99.8 percent of all the mass in the Solar System. The rest flattened into a spinning disk where dust grains stuck together and grew into the planets, moons, asteroids and comets that orbit the Sun today.

The Sun is a medium-sized star made mostly of hydrogen and helium. In its core, where the temperature reaches about 15 million degrees Celsius, nuclear fusion turns hydrogen into helium and releases enormous amounts of energy. The visible surface is much cooler, at around 5,500 degrees Celsius. Sunlight takes about 8 minutes and 20 seconds to travel the 150 million kilometres to Earth.

Mercury is the smallest planet and the closest one to the Sun. It has almost no atmosphere to trap heat, so its temperature swings from about 430 degrees Celsius during the day to minus 180 degrees Celsius at night. Mercury completes one orbit around the Sun in just 88 Earth days, the shortest year of any planet, and its grey surface is covered in craters like the Moon.

Venus is the hottest planet, even though Mercury is closer to the Sun. Its thick atmosphere of carbon dioxide traps heat in a runaway greenhouse effect, keeping the surface at around 465 degrees Celsius, hot enough to melt lead. Venus also spins backwards compared with most planets, and it rotates so slowly that one day on Venus, 243 Earth days, is longer than its year of 225 Earth days.

Earth is the only planet known to support life. About 71 percent of its surface is covered by water, and its atmosphere is made of roughly 78 percent nitrogen and 21 percent oxygen. Earth has one natural satellite, the Moon, which most scientists believe formed from the debris of a giant collision between the young Earth and a Mars-sized body.

Mars is known as the Red Planet because iron oxide, or rust, covers its surface. It is home to Olympus Mons, the largest volcano in the Solar System, which rises roughly 22 kilometres high, about two and a half times the height of Mount Everest. Mars has two small moons, Phobos and Deimos. Robotic rovers such as Curiosity, which landed in 2012, and Perseverance, which landed in 2021, explore its surface for signs of ancient water and life.

Between the orbits of Mars and Jupiter lies the asteroid belt, a region filled with rocky objects left over from the formation of the Solar System. The largest object in the belt is Ceres, about 940 kilometres across, which is classified as a dwarf planet. Although films often show asteroids packed closely together, the objects in the belt are usually separated by hundreds of thousands of kilometres.

Jupiter is the largest planet, with more than twice the mass of all the other planets combined. It is a gas giant made mostly of hydrogen and helium, and its Great Red Spot is a storm larger than the whole Earth that has been observed for centuries. Its four largest moons, Io, Europa, Ganymede and Callisto, were discovered by Galileo Galilei in 1610. Ganymede is the largest moon in the Solar System and is even bigger than the planet Mercury.

Saturn is famous for its spectacular rings, which are made mostly of chunks of ice and rock ranging from tiny grains to pieces as large as houses. Saturn is so light for its size that its average density is lower than that of water. Its largest moon, Titan, has a thick atmosphere and lakes of liquid methane and ethane on its surface. The Cassini spacecraft studied Saturn and its moons from 2004 until 2017.

Uranus and Neptune are known as ice giants because they contain large amounts of water, ammonia and methane ices. Uranus is tilted by about 98 degrees, so it spins on its side as it travels around the Sun. Neptune, the farthest planet, has the fastest winds in the Solar System, reaching more than 2,000 kilometres per hour. Neptune was the first planet found by mathematical prediction rather than by observation, and it was confirmed through a telescope in 1846.

Beyond Neptune lies the Kuiper Belt, a ring of icy bodies that includes Pluto. Pluto was considered the ninth planet until 2006, when the International Astronomical Union reclassified it as a dwarf planet. In July 2015 the New Horizons spacecraft flew past Pluto and photographed a large heart-shaped plain of nitrogen ice. Much farther out, the Oort Cloud is thought to be the source of long-period comets.

Space probes have explored every planet in the Solar System. Voyager 1, launched by NASA in 1977, flew past Jupiter and Saturn and in 2012 became the first human-made object to enter interstellar space. It is now the most distant spacecraft from Earth and still sends data back, even though its signals take more than 20 hours to reach us.
