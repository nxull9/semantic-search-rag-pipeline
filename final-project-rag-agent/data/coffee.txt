Coffee comes from the seeds of a flowering plant that grows naturally in the highlands of Ethiopia. A popular legend tells of a goat herder named Kaldi who noticed that his goats became unusually energetic after eating the red berries of a certain bush. Whether or not the story is true, Ethiopia is widely recognised as the birthplace of coffee, and wild coffee plants still grow in its forests today.

The first people known to cultivate and brew coffee as a drink were in Yemen in the fifteenth century. Sufi communities drank it to stay awake during long nights of prayer. Coffee was shipped from the Yemeni port of Mocha, and the name of that port is still used today for chocolate-flavoured coffee drinks.

From Yemen, coffee spread to Mecca, Cairo and Istanbul, where public coffee houses became popular meeting places for conversation, music and games. In the seventeenth century coffee reached Europe through the port of Venice. Coffee houses in London were nicknamed penny universities because, for the price of a cup, people could join lively discussions. The famous insurance market Lloyd's of London began in a coffee house owned by Edward Lloyd.

There are two main species of coffee. Arabica, or Coffea arabica, makes up roughly 60 percent of world production and is known for its smooth, sweet and aromatic flavour. It grows best at high altitudes and is more delicate. Robusta, or Coffea canephora, is hardier, grows at lower altitudes and contains about twice as much caffeine, which gives it a stronger and more bitter taste. Robusta is often used in instant coffee and espresso blends.

Coffee grows in the so-called coffee belt, the tropical region between the Tropic of Cancer and the Tropic of Capricorn. A coffee plant usually takes three to four years to produce its first harvest. Its fruit is called a coffee cherry, and each cherry normally contains two seeds, which are the coffee beans. Sometimes a cherry holds only one round seed, known as a peaberry.

Brazil is by far the largest coffee producer in the world, growing around a third of all the world's coffee. Vietnam is the second largest producer and grows mainly Robusta. Other major producers include Colombia, Indonesia and Ethiopia. Coffee is one of the most traded agricultural products in the world, and people drink an estimated two billion cups every day.

After harvesting, the beans must be separated from the fruit. In the washed, or wet, process the fruit is removed with water before the beans are dried, which produces a clean and bright taste. In the natural, or dry, process the whole cherries are dried in the sun with the beans inside, giving a fruitier and heavier flavour. The honey process sits in between, leaving some of the sticky fruit on the beans while they dry.

Green coffee beans have little flavour until they are roasted at temperatures of around 200 to 230 degrees Celsius. During roasting, chemical reactions such as the Maillard reaction and caramelisation create hundreds of aroma compounds. Light roasts keep more of the bean's natural acidity and origin flavours, while dark roasts taste more bitter, smoky and chocolatey. Contrary to popular belief, a dark roast does not contain more caffeine than a light roast.

There are many ways to brew coffee. Espresso forces hot water through finely ground coffee at about 9 bars of pressure for 25 to 30 seconds, producing a small, concentrated shot topped with crema. A French press steeps coarse grounds in hot water for about four minutes before pressing them down with a metal filter. Pour-over methods drip water slowly through a paper filter, and cold brew steeps grounds in cold water for 12 to 24 hours. Most experts recommend water at 90 to 96 degrees Celsius for hot brewing.

The main active ingredient in coffee is caffeine. A typical cup of brewed coffee contains about 95 milligrams of caffeine. Caffeine keeps people alert by blocking adenosine, a chemical in the brain that builds up during the day and makes us feel sleepy. Health authorities generally consider up to 400 milligrams of caffeine a day to be safe for most healthy adults.

Arabic coffee, known as qahwa, has a special place in the culture of Saudi Arabia and the Arabian Peninsula. It is made from lightly roasted beans brewed with cardamom, and sometimes saffron or cloves, then served in small handleless cups called finjan, usually with dates. Serving coffee is a symbol of generosity and hospitality, and UNESCO added Arabic coffee to its list of the Intangible Cultural Heritage of Humanity in 2015.

Saudi Arabia also grows its own coffee. In the mountains of the Jazan region in the southwest, farmers cultivate Khawlani coffee on terraced slopes, a tradition passed down for generations. Saudi Arabia named 2022 the Year of Saudi Coffee to celebrate this heritage, and in the same year UNESCO recognised the knowledge and practices related to cultivating Khawlani coffee beans.
